SLIDE 1 — Titolo
[ ~30 secondi — introduzione personale ]

Cosa dire
Good morning. My project is an end-to-end Data Warehouse built on the Olist Brazilian e-commerce dataset — around 100,000 real, anonymised orders from 2016 to 2018.
The goal was to build a decision-support system that answers concrete business questions: which product categories generate the most revenue, where delivery delays concentrate geographically, and how sales evolved over time.
I’ll walk you through the full pipeline — from raw CSV files to four interactive Tableau dashboards.


SLIDE 2 — Dataset & Business Questions
[ ~1 minuto ]

Cosa dire
Olist is a Brazilian marketplace connecting small sellers to large e-commerce platforms. The dataset, publicly available on Kaggle, consists of nine relational CSV files: customers, sellers, orders, order items, payments, reviews, products, geolocation, and a category-name translation table.
The five business questions that drive the entire architecture are:
•  Which product categories generate the most revenue — and how is it distributed geographically?
•  How have sales evolved from 2016 to 2018? Are there seasonal peaks?
•  How does customer satisfaction vary by category, state, and seller?
•  What percentage of orders are delivered on time, and where are the worst delays?
•  Which payment method dominates by volume and value?

Se chiedono perché Olist
Dataset reale, multi-tabella, ricco di sfide di qualità dei dati. Perfetto per dimostrare l’intera pipeline DW end-to-end.


SLIDE 3 — Project Pipeline
[ ~1 minuto ]

Cosa dire
The pipeline has seven sequential steps.
•  Step 1: Data Collection — nine raw CSV files from Kaggle.
•  Step 2: ER Schema — I designed a reconciled relational database consolidating all sources into a normalised, consistent structure.
•  Step 3: DQA — a programmatic scorecard across five quality indices, augmented by a local LLM.
•  Step 4: Cleaning — deduplication, normalisation, imputation, outlier flagging.
•  Step 5: DFM and Star Schema design — dimensional fact model, then translation to a star schema.
•  Step 6: ETL — load into SQLite (olist_dw.db). Referential integrity verified with zero orphan rows.
•  Step 7: Visualisation — four Tableau dashboards demonstrating all five OLAP operations.

Se chiedono perché SQLite e non PostgreSQL
SQLite è embedded, portabile, zero configurazione server. Per un dataset di questa dimensione (112.650 righe) è più che sufficiente. PostgreSQL sarebbe necessario per ambienti multi-utente o carichi di produzione.


SLIDE 4 — Data Quality Analysis
[ ~2 minuti — sezione tecnica importante ]

Cosa dire
The DQA phase uses a programmatic scorecard — a Python class called DQAReport — that evaluates five quality indices on declarative rules.
•  Validity: percentage of values that satisfy domain constraints — types, ranges, whitelists.
•  Completeness: percentage of non-null values on mandatory attributes.
•  Uniqueness: percentage of records with a unique primary key.
•  Consistency: cross-attribute coherence — for example, delivery date cannot precede purchase date.
•  Precision: percentage of values within mean ±3σ — identifies statistical outliers.

Six problem types were identified across the dataset: missing values, wrong values, noisy data, FK violations, record duplication, and varying value representations.

For null classification, I used the MCAR–MAR–MNAR taxonomy. For example: delivered_customer_date is null for unshipped orders — that’s MAR, explained by order_status. Review comment text is null because customers chose not to write one — that’s a semantic null, not a missing value.

L’LLM locale
At three progressive levels: L1 produces a human-readable narrative of quality issues per table. L2 classifies each missing value as MCAR, MAR, or MNAR with a justification. L3 generates a prioritised cleaning plan before any intervention. All runs through llama3.2:3b via Ollama — fully offline, zero data transmission.

Se chiedono perché locale
Privacy: i dati non escono dall’infrastruttura. In un contesto aziendale reale questo è un requisito non negoziabile.

Se chiedono perché IQR e non ±3σ
IQR è più robusto in presenza di outlier estremi: media e deviazione standard sono esse stesse influenzate dagli stessi valori anomali che si cerca di individuare. Con distribuzioni asimmetriche come price o weight_g, IQR identifica meglio i valori fuori range.


SLIDE 5 — Design Decisions
[ ~1.5 minuti — slide molto tecnica, aspettarsi domande ]

Cosa dire
Six key choices shaped the dimensional model.

01 — Granularità: Order Item, non Order
The fact table records each individual order item — the finest available granularity. A single order can contain products from different categories and sellers: using Order as the fact would collapse all that detail. With Order Item I can always aggregate up, but I can’t disaggregate down.

02 — Pruning: customer_unique_id
Removed from the attribute tree. At the order-item level, customer_id is already sufficient to identify the customer. customer_unique_id — which maps multiple customer_ids to the same physical person — is redundant at this granularity and adds no analytical value.

03 — Grafting: zip_code_prefix
The zip_code_prefix node was removed from the hierarchy, but its children — city and state — were reconnected directly to customer_id. We lose ZIP-level analysis, which is too fine for the planned queries, but we keep the meaningful geographic hierarchy city → state.

04 — Measure Additivity
price and freight_value are fully additive — summable across all dimensions. delivery_lead_days is semi-additive — AVG and MAX make sense, SUM does not. review_score is non-additive — only AVG is semantically valid. payment_value is additive only at order level.

05 — Payment Value: Double-Counting
payment_value is pre-aggregated per order (SUM of all payment methods) and then replicated on every item row. If you SUM across the entire fact table you get R$19.23M instead of the correct R$15.38M. Always aggregate by order_id first, or use SUM(price) for revenue — that’s always additive.

06 — LLM Locale
Chose llama3.2:3b via Ollama over cloud APIs for data privacy and reproducibility. Zero cost, zero data transmission, results are deterministic.

Se chiedono perché Star e non Snowflake
Star Schema denormalizza le gerarchie nelle dimensioni: meno JOIN, aggregazioni più veloci per le query OLAP di Tableau. I dati del DW sono storici e immutabili, quindi la ridondanza non è un problema operativo.


SLIDE 6 — Star Schema
[ ~1 minuto — mostrare il diagramma, poi ETL ]

Cosa dire
The star schema has one central fact table — fact_sales, 112,650 rows — and five dimension tables.
•  dim_date: 634 rows, generated synthetically from purchase timestamps. date_id = YYYYMMDD integer — sortable, readable, efficient for joins.
•  dim_product: 32,951 rows. Includes category_name_english, added via join with the translation table during cleaning.
•  dim_customer: 99,441 rows. Enriched with lat/lng from the geolocation centroid via LEFT JOIN on zip_code_prefix.
•  dim_seller: 3,095 rows. Same geographic enrichment.
•  dim_payment_type: 5 rows — the only dimension with a surrogate key, because payment_type values are plain text strings with no natural numeric key.

fact_sales contains two degenerate attributes — order_id and order_item_id — for traceability to the source, without a dedicated dimension.
Referential integrity was verified with explicit control queries post-load: zero orphan rows toward any dimension. Three NULL values in payment_type_id correspond to cancelled orders with no registered payment — not FK violations, but legitimate operational state.

Se chiedono surrogate key
Ho usato surrogate key per fact_sales (INT PK: sale_id) e per dim_payment_type (payment_type_id intero). Per le altre dimensioni ho mantenuto le chiavi naturali per tracciabilità diretta con la sorgente.

SLIDE 11 — Key Findings
[ ~1 minuto — sintesi dei 6 risultati ]

Cosa dire
Six main findings emerged from the full pipeline.
•  Delivery: 71% on time, 12-day median. Northern states take 3× longer than São Paulo — a structural logistical gap.
•  Revenue: R$13 million from 98,666 orders. health_beauty is the dominant category, credit card the dominant payment method.
•  Geography: the São Paulo–Rio–Minas triangle accounts for two-thirds of all volume.
•  Growth: +191% in one year, with a Black Friday spike of 7,451 items in November 2017.
•  Satisfaction: median 4/5. Right-skewed distribution — most customers are satisfied, but one-star reviews cluster around late deliveries.
•  Data quality: the DQA phase caught the payment double-counting (+25%), the miskeyed review records (814 records with a composite key issue), and 261,831 duplicate geolocation rows — all of which would have silently corrupted downstream analysis.


SLIDE 12 — Conclusions
[ ~45 secondi — chiusura ]

Cosa dire
Three takeaways.
First: data quality is a prerequisite, not an afterthought. Without the DQA phase, every downstream analysis would have been quietly wrong.
Second: geography explains everything in this dataset. The SP–RJ–MG triangle dominates volume, revenue, and seller density. Northern Brazil faces delivery times three times longer — a structural gap that no dashboard optimisation can fix.
Third: the growth is real — and the platform over-promises delivery windows. The 12-day buffer versus estimated dates is a risk, not a feature.

Two planned extensions: a macro-category mapping table for products (the source has no lookup), and a bridge table for orders with multiple payment methods to eliminate the current replication artefact.

Thank you. I’m happy to take questions.


APPENDICE — Risposte Rapide alle Domande Più Probabili

Perché Star Schema e non Snowflake?
Meno JOIN, aggregazioni più veloci. Le gerarchie sono denormalizzate nelle dimensioni. Dati storici immutabili: la ridondanza non è un problema. Per query OLAP in Tableau, Star è la scelta standard.

Perché Order Item e non Order come fatto?
L’Order Item è la granularità più fine. Un ordine può contenere prodotti di categorie diverse e venditori diversi. Con Order Item posso sempre aggregare verso l’ordine, ma non potrei scendere.

Perché non hai implementato macro_category e region?
La sorgente non include il mapping categoria→macro-categoria né stato→regione. Implementarle richiederebbe classificare soggettivamente ~70 categorie e mappare manualmente 27 stati. Estensioni pianificate.

La bridge table: perché non l’hai fatta?
Avrebbe richiesto un secondo fatto nel DW, complicando le query Tableau. Ho adottato la pre-aggregazione per ordine: documentato come limitazione nota. Soluzione corretta per una versione futura.

Perché non hai corretto il CAP a 4 cifre?
Richiederebbe consultare il database CEP ufficiale dei Correios brasiliani. Non disponibile nella sorgente. Nessun impatto sulle analisi a livello città/stato. Documentato come limite noto.

Come mai review_id non è PK?
789 valori di review_id sono associati a 2+ order_id distinti. La PK corretta è (review_id, order_id). Deduplicare solo su review_id avrebbe rimosso erroneamente 814 record legittimi.

SUM(payment_value) dà 19.23M o 15.38M?
19.23M — sbagliato. payment_value è replicato su ogni item dello stesso ordine. Il valore corretto (15.38M) si ottiene aggregando per order_id prima di sommare. Per il revenue uso SUM(price) che non ha questo problema.

Cosa faresti diversamente?
Bridge table per i pagamenti multipli, lookup table per macro_category e region, dataset più recente (post-2020). Stessa architettura, più completa.
